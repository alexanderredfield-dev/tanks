# The issue: Toxic Tank Farms

You see them every day, driving across the bridge or running on the Greenbelt or walking your kids to school. And perhaps you smell them: the tanks. They are a prominent part of the city landscape, 120 massive aboveground petroleum storage tanks from Bug Light to Cash Corner, located within our neighborhoods and adjacent to parks, playgrounds, day cares, senior housing and businesses. 

In 2019, South Portland residents learned that two petroleum tank farm operators, Global and Sprague, had been violating their permit conditions under the Clean Air Act for years, in a big way. [The city and state knew](https://www.pressherald.com/2019/10/16/epa-rejects-south-portland-arguments-sticks-with-original-penalties-against-tank-farm-owner/) and did nothing. So the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, under President Obama, sued. Those lawsuits ended in a settlement that did not mandate air quality improvements or ensure community safety, but they did set off years of debate and testing from which we finally have some results.  

After years of advocacy by Protect South Portland and concerned residents, the Legislature mandated that the tank farm operators maintain ongoing monitoring of the air quality at their fencelines. The first year of data was released this year, showing that the air around two tanks farms in particular—Citgo on Mechanic Street, and South Portland Terminal on Lincoln Street—routinely exceeded state standards for benzene, a known carcinogen, and often dramatically exceeded it. Ambient air monitoring conducted by the state Department of Environmental Protection (DEP) has consistently shown benzene above the guidelines at the Pearl Street station adjacent to Citgo for two years now also. The DEP, which issues air permits for the tanks, has the regulatory power to require the tank farms to fully test and regulate their emissions. It’s time for them to do so. **It’s time for them to stop letting the tanks profit while dumping their waste in this community.**

**Background and Context**  

Taken together, the tanks in South Portland are allowed, under their Maine DEP licenses, to emit approximatelyn 600 tons of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) each year and approximately 100 tons per year of hazardous air pollutants (HAPs), a category that includes benzene. These tanks are located in close proximity to each other and are flanked by single-family homes, senior housing facilities, schools, day care, playgrounds, public parks and businesses. 

Of the six tank farms in South Portland, half are designated as “minor” emitters and half are designated “major” emitters, with major emitters generally being subject to more scrutiny of their operations and required mitigation. However, while Citgo and South Portland Terminal are both designated “major” emitters, they are essentially given waivers from regulations aimed at controlling HAPs. That’s because the companies themselves report that they don’t emit enough HAPs to meet the threshold at which such technology would be required. The companies came to that conclusion using formulas created by the American Petroleum Institute, a powerful industry lobby group. 

But we know that those formulas have proved faulty. In fact, in 2011, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency began investigating tank farms across New England, over concerns that they were underreporting their emissions. 

In Maine, the EPA focused on tank farms owned by Global (South Portland) and Sprague (South Portland and Searsport). In 2012 and 2013, the EPA required the companies to hire third-party evaluators to actually test the emissions at their heated storage tanks in South Portland and Searsport. Those tests revealed that Global and Sprague were in serious violation of their state permits and the Clean Air Act, and resulted in the EPA issuing notices of violation. When the state effectively ignored the EPA’s findings and continued to renew the companies’ licenses with a “synthetic minor emitter” designation, the EPA sued the tank farm operators, saying that they were in fact “major” emitters, should be licensed as such and subject to strong emissions control requirements.

In 2019, those lawsuits resulted in consent decrees with [Sprague](https://www.southportland.gov/DocumentCenter/View/4011/EPA-Settlement-with-Sprague-Fact-Sheet) and [Global](https://www.epa.gov/newsreleases/proposed-settlement-resolves-clean-air-act-claims-between-epa-and-south-portland-maine) that included fines and installation of vapor control technology, but that had no meaningful provisions for the monitoring or mitigation of hazardous air pollutants. They did not ease residents’ concerns.

In the wake of those lawsuits, the city partnered with the Maine DEP to use new and existing [ambient air quality monitoring](https://www3.maine.gov/dep/air/monitoring/spo-voc-monitor.html) stations to track air quality around the city. However, the state refused to locate the monitors near tanks that were embedded in residential neighborhoods, citing logistical challenges. The monitor closest to a tank farm was at Bug Light, somewhat near to tanks but not adjacent to the houses most affected by them. That data found evidence of “burst emissions,” created when tankers come in and offload their product. Movement of the petroleum products from ships to pipes and into tanks provides opportunities for fumes to escape, especially as the tanks are filled and the air inside the tank is pushed out. Some tanks have a floating roof, meant to move with the product and mitigate that large output all at once. In the first years of the monitoring program, the monitoring station at Bug Light captured wide variations in the benzene emissions, sometimes several-fold higher than the state's Ambient Air Guidelines. DEP [analysts examined those results](https://www3.maine.gov/dep/air/monitoring/docs/archive/MECDC-CAAC-24hr-voc-sample-analysis-update11.09.20-annotated.pdf) in conjunction with wind direction and weather at the time and the state meteorologist indicated that the tanks nearby were the sources of those benzene bursts. The meteorologist later backpedaled from that assertion. In 2020, the EPA sued Gulf Oil (owner of a tank farm near Bug Light) for violations in its emissions permits. That resulted in a civil penalty of \$2,400,000.  
   
Meanwhile, the city had established its own committee to review air quality and “to present the South Portland City Council with timely yet comprehensive recommendations on improving air quality throughout South Portland.” The Clean Air Advisory Committee’s work spanned 13 months and resulted in a [final report](https://www.southportland.gov/DocumentCenter/View/1105/Clean-Air-Advisory-Committee-CAAC-Report-and-Recommendations---March-2-2021-PDF) in February 2021\. The committee called on the city to work with EPA and DEP to “achieve better air quality data in neighborhoods most impacted by emitters.” The recommendations included actually measuring emissions (instead of relying on tank farm operators’ own estimates) and pushing the DEP for stricter regulatory and licensing requirements, including requiring the tanks to use emissions control technology that’s already available on the market. The city is still working through ways to take meaningful action on the [recommendations of that committee](https://www.southportland.gov/DocumentCenter/View/1105/Clean-Air-Advisory-Committee-CAAC-Report-and-Recommendations---March-2-2021-PDF), which it created. 

Protect South Portland joined in 2021 with 11 other health and environmental advocacy groups to ask the legislature to require tank farms to continuously monitor the air quality at their fencelines, and to require twice-yearly testing of heated petroleum tanks. [That bill](https://legislature.maine.gov/legis/bills/getPDF.asp?paper=HP0119&item=2&snum=130) was signed into law in 2023\. 

In 2023, the DEP provided a [health analysis of the air quality monitoring results to date](https://www3.maine.gov/dep/air/monitoring/docs/archive/2yr%20Summary%20Report%20June%202023.pdf) but the data didn’t include the fenceline monitoring nor the new Pearl Street station data (next to Citgo), and they remained unconcerned about the potential for burst emissions. Residents once again called for the DEP to act. The state’s response was to give it time and more ambient air monitoring in the same manner they had been doing. 

As a result of the 2023 bill, the tanks farms now maintain systems that continuously monitor air quality at their fencelines, reporting out readings that average air quality over two-week periods for four VOCs: benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylenes. The first year of fenceline [testing results were released in 2025](https://www1.maine.gov/dep//air/licensing/ch171fenceline.html) and a subsequent analysis is forthcoming as of this writing in early October 2026\. 

Concerning readings were collected near all the tanks, but two tank farms’ fenceline readings stand out. At the Pearl Street monitor on Citgo fenceline, which sits very close to homes in Pleasantdale neighborhood, monitors found the air consistently and dramatically exceeded state standards for benzene, a well-studied carcinogen. The highest benzene measurements were at South Portland Terminal (Buckeye) on Lincoln Street, bordered by businesses and Forest City Cemetery, with the average benzene there coming in  at more than double the state’s ambient air quality standard, which is meant to protect people from cancer risk. The highest single reading  at SP Terminal was 12 times higher than that standard. 

The state released a [report in September 2026](https://mobile.maine.gov/dep/air/monitoring/docs/archive/South%20Portland%20-%20Portland%2024%20Hour%20VOC%20Air%20Monitoring%206yr%20Report%20Update_FINAL_09.08.26.pdf) summarizing findings from ambient air monitoring stations located across South Portland and Portland. The 2026 DEP analysis talks about not being concerned regarding health effects from short-term exposure to benzene, but we all should be concerned about the long-term exposure for people who live among the tank farms. Benzene is a known human carcinogen, and long-term exposure damages the bone marrow and is causally linked to leukemia and other blood disorders, with the World Health Organization stating there is no known safe level of exposure. Children are especially vulnerable because they breathe more air relative to their body weight and their blood-forming systems are still developing, and older adults and people with weakened immune systems may also be less able to tolerate the damage. Maine's ambient air guideline is meant to protect people from such risks, and it is being violated. State regulators must act. 

For years, the tank farms have operated with little regulatory oversight. We know that when they have been measured, they were found to have grossly underestimated their effect on the surrounding neighborhoods. Now we know that the air around the tank farms is bad. We know enough to act. 

 

**Here’s what we’re asking the City Council to do:**

 

* **Demand that the DEP use its full authority as the state’s licensing body** to recognize that tanks as major polluters and regulate them accordingly. DEP has tools at its disposal and the City Council should pressure the agency to use them:  
  * Require major emitters, including Citgo and South Portland Terminal, to actually control their emissions. Both are granted waivers on requirements related to Hazardous Air Pollutants based on promises that they don’t emit enough to meet the thresholds for those controls. The fenceline data calls those assumptions into question. DEP should require those facilities to test their emissions and install controls, as the regulations allow them to do.  
  * Consider the fenceline results in *all* future licensing actions. State regulation specifically states the burden is on emitters to prove that their operations will not violate ambient air quality standards, and that new information about ambient air quality must be taken into consideration when a license comes up for renewal.   
  * Create a “source aggregation” framework to consider the cumulative effects of the tanks farms, located in close proximity to one another, when licensing any individual tank farm in South Portland.

* **Call on the DEP to fulfill its charge of environmental protection.** The state’s own emissions standards are being violated. *Many* South Portlanders live close to tank farms and, more picnic at Bug Light, run on the Greenbelt, or drop their children off each day at daycares or schools close to the tanks. We know enough that failing to act to protect this community would be a clear dereliction of duty by the DEP. The city council should say so.  
    
* **Stop the tank farms from dumping pollutants in our neighborhoods.**
